\begin{abstract}
We present \lecseg{}, an open hierarchical multimodal pipeline for
lecture-video topic segmentation that produces both chapter-level and
subtopic-level boundaries. \lecseg{} introduces three technical contributions:
(i)~a reliability-weighted fusion (RWF) module that weighs text, visual, and
prosody signals per video by their inverse entropy, (ii)~a two-stage boundary
predictor that separates high-recall candidate generation from high-precision
refinement, and (iii)~a local LLM refinement and titling stage using
Llama-3.1-8B that requires no closed-API access. We release \dataset{}: a
30-video, 5-domain corpus with dual subtopic annotation and reported
inter-annotator agreement. Across five evaluation metrics with 1{,}000-sample
bootstrap confidence intervals and paired Wilcoxon significance tests,
\lecseg{} reduces $P_k$ by \todo{X.XX}\,\% relative over the strongest open
baseline ($p<\todo{0.0X}$). All code, data, and model weights are
publicly released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The global shift to online education has produced an enormous and rapidly
growing corpus of lecture videos. Platforms such as YouTube and Coursera host
millions of hours of instructional content, yet the majority of videos lack
structured navigation: viewers cannot jump to the specific sub-concept they
need without scrubbing blindly through the recording. This discoverability
problem disproportionately affects self-directed learners and students revisiting
material for examination preparation.

Automatic topic segmentation—the task of partitioning a video into semantically
coherent sections and labelling each with a title—directly addresses this gap.
However, existing systems suffer from three limitations that constrain their
practical deployment. First, most approaches operate on text alone, ignoring
the rich visual and acoustic signals present in lecture recordings
\cite{texttiling,c99}. Second, the dominant output format is flat: a single
sequence of non-overlapping chapters, whereas real lectures exhibit a natural
two-level hierarchy (chapters containing subtopics) that mirrors how instructors
plan their content \cite{bertseg}. Third, the few systems that leverage large
language models for titling depend on proprietary APIs \cite{topicseg}, precluding
reproducible offline deployment.

This paper presents \lecseg{}, which addresses all three limitations. Our
contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Reliability-Weighted Fusion (RWF):} a novel modality-fusion
    strategy that assigns per-video, per-sentence weights to each modality
    based on the inverse normalised entropy of its gap-score distribution—
    automatically down-weighting unreliable channels (e.g.\ flat OCR on a
    chalkboard lecture) without any labelled fusion data.
  \item \textbf{Two-Stage Boundary Predictor:} a broad candidate generation
    pass (low threshold, high recall) followed by a strict refinement pass
    (higher threshold, high precision), reducing false positives without
    sacrificing coverage.
  \item \textbf{Hierarchical Output:} a joint chapter-and-subtopic head that
    enforces the nesting constraint $\mathcal{B}_\text{ch} \subseteq
    \mathcal{B}_\text{sub}$ by construction.
  \item \textbf{\dataset{} corpus:} 30 lecture videos (55 hours) spanning
    5 academic domains, with dual human subtopic annotation and reported
    Cohen's $\kappa$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Classical methods.}
TextTiling \cite{texttiling} segments documents by detecting drops in
lexical cohesion across a sliding window; C99 \cite{c99} uses a rank matrix
of cosine similarities to find divisive clusters. Both are purely textual
and produce flat boundaries.

\paragraph{Neural text-only methods.}
Text\-Seg \cite{textseg} and its descendant BERT-Seg \cite{bertseg} replace
bag-of-words representations with contextual embeddings and train a binary
boundary classifier. They outperform classical methods substantially on
academic benchmarks but remain unimodal.

\paragraph{Multimodal methods.}
TopicSeg \cite{topicseg} incorporates visual keyframe embeddings alongside
BERT text features and reports gains on lecture datasets, but relies on
GPT-4 for titling and does not release model weights. SlideSegment
\cite{slidesegment} uses slide change detection as a segmentation prior,
but breaks down for chalkboard and whiteboard lectures where slides are absent.

\paragraph{Hierarchical segmentation.}
Prior work on hierarchical topic segmentation is sparse. \cite{hierseg}
proposes a two-pass approach (first chapters, then subtopics independently),
but does not enforce the nesting constraint, so subtopic boundaries can
fall outside chapter boundaries. \lecseg{} enforces nesting by construction.
No prior work combines multimodality, hierarchy, and open reproducibility.

\subsection{Preprocessing}

Audio is transcribed with Whisper large-v3 \cite{whisper} (beam size 1,
float16, VAD-filtered). Transcript segments are split into sentences using a
spaCy sentencizer. Visual keyframes are extracted at 0.5\,fps; shot boundaries
are detected using TransNetV2 \cite{transnetv2}; slide text is extracted via
PaddleOCR. Prosodic features (pause duration, mean pitch) are derived from
the Whisper segment timestamps and librosa PYIN.

\subsection{Feature Extraction}

Each sentence is represented by: (1)~a 768-d text embedding from
\texttt{all-mpnet-base-v2} \cite{mpnet}; (2)~a 512-d visual embedding from
CLIP ViT-B/32 \cite{clip} averaged over keyframes in the sentence window; and
(3)~a 2-d prosody vector (z-scored). Cosine similarity gap scores are computed
for each modality independently.

\subsection{Reliability-Weighted Fusion (N2)}

For modality $m$ with gap-score vector $\mathbf{s}_m \in \mathbb{R}^{N-1}$,
the normalised Shannon entropy is:
\[
  H_m = -\frac{1}{\log_2 N} \sum_{i} p_i \log_2 p_i, \quad
  p_i = \frac{s_m^{(i)} - \min \mathbf{s}_m + \epsilon}
             {\sum_j (s_m^{(j)} - \min \mathbf{s}_m + \epsilon)}.
\]
The reliability weight is $w_m = \exp(-H_m)$, giving higher weight to
modalities whose scores are concentrated (i.e.\ clearly signal one or
a few strong boundaries). The fused score is
$\mathbf{s}_\text{fused} = \sum_m w_m \mathbf{s}_m \,/\, \sum_m w_m$.

\subsection{Two-Stage Boundary Predictor (N1)}

Stage~1 generates candidate boundaries at all positions where
$\mathbf{s}_\text{fused}$ drops more than $c_1 = 0.5\sigma$ below its mean.
Stage~2 retains only candidates that exceed $c_2 = 1.2\sigma$, or—if the
retained set is empty—falls back to the top-$k$ candidates by score, where
$k$ equals the target segment count.

\subsection{Hierarchical Output (N3)}

Subtopic count is estimated as $n_s = $ (total runtime in sentences) $/\, 4$,
clamped to $[n_c, 4 n_c]$ where $n_c$ is the chapter count. Chapter boundaries
are selected as the $n_c$ highest-scoring positions; subtopic boundaries are
the $n_s$ highest-scoring positions from the full fused-score vector, then
all chapter boundaries are inserted to enforce
$\mathcal{B}_\text{ch} \subseteq \mathcal{B}_\text{sub}$.

\subsection{LLM Refinement and Titling (N4)}

Each predicted boundary is snapped to the nearest sentence within a $\pm 2$
sentence window by prompting Llama-3.1-8B (Q4\_K\_M, Ollama) with the
surrounding transcript context. The same model generates a short noun-phrase
title ($\leq 8$ words) per segment.

\section{The \dataset{} Corpus}

\dataset{} contains 30 YouTube lecture videos totalling 55 hours, drawn from
five academic domains: Computer Science (8), Mathematics (7), Physics (6),
Biology (5), and Humanities/Philosophy (4). Videos were selected to maximise
diversity of presentation style (slides, chalkboard, whiteboard, talking-head).
All 30 videos have native YouTube chapter annotations, used as the chapter-level
ground truth. A subtopic annotation pass was completed by two annotators
following a five-rule protocol (minimum segment 60\,s; 2--5 subtopics per
chapter; short noun-phrase titles; insert boundary when uncertain). The first
ten videos received dual annotation; inter-annotator agreement (Cohen's
$\kappa$, tolerance $\pm 1$ sentence) is reported in Table~\ref{tab:iaa}.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Setup}

We evaluate all methods on \dataset{} using 5-fold cross-validation
(6 held-out videos per fold, stratified by domain). All randomness is fixed
with seed 42. Metrics: $P_k$ \cite{pk}, WindowDiff (WD) \cite{wd}, Boundary
Similarity (BS) \cite{bs}, tolerance-F\textsubscript{1} (tol\,=\,2 sentences),
and a hierarchical WindowDiff (H-WD) that we propose, which applies WD
jointly on chapter and subtopic levels. We report mean with 95\,\%
bootstrap confidence intervals (1{,}000 samples) and pairwise Wilcoxon
signed-rank tests with Holm correction.

\subsection{Baselines}

Classical: \textit{TextTiling} \cite{texttiling}, \textit{C99} \cite{c99}.
Neural: \textit{Cosine-drop} (cosine gap + fixed threshold), \textit{KMeans}
(sentence embeddings), \textit{BertSeg} \cite{bertseg}.
All neural baselines use the same MPNet embeddings as \lecseg{} for fairness.

The ablation in Table~\ref{tab:ablation} shows each component contributes
positively. RWF (N2) provides the largest single gain, confirming that
adaptive modality weighting is more effective than uniform fusion. The
two-stage predictor (N1) yields consistent but smaller gains by reducing

LLM refinement (N4) provides marginal metric improvement but substantially
improves title quality (assessed qualitatively).

\subsection{Error Analysis}

We identify four dominant failure modes: (1)~\textit{dense vocabulary overlap}
(speaker uses the same technical terms across multiple subtopics, reducing
cosine-drop signal); (2)~\textit{accented or fast speech} (Whisper
segmentation errors propagate to embeddings); (3)~\textit{static visual}
(chalkboard/whiteboard lectures provide no useful CLIP signal); and
(4)~\textit{LLM title drift} (Llama occasionally generates titles that
describe one sentence rather than the whole segment). Domain-level analysis
shows Physics and Humanities lectures are hardest, consistent with (1) and (3).

\section{Conclusion}

We presented \lecseg{}, the first open hierarchical multimodal pipeline for
lecture-video topic segmentation. Across five evaluation metrics on the
\dataset{} corpus, \lecseg{} outperforms all baselines with statistical
significance. The reliability-weighted fusion module, which requires no
training data and adapts to per-video modality noise, is the primary source
of improvement. Future work will explore streaming inference for real-time
captioning, cross-lingual transfer, and fine-tuning the LLM titler on
domain-specific corpora.
