\begin{abstract}
We present \lecseg{}, a low-resource, reproducible pipeline for lecture-video
topic segmentation that produces chapter-level and subtopic-level structure.
\lecseg{} contributes a 30-video, 32.52-hour lecture benchmark, a unified
evaluation suite, and a family of text, multimodal, and method-selection
segmentation experiments. The strongest statistically supported 30-video
result reduces $P_k$ from 0.3884 to 0.3713 and WindowDiff from 0.3956 to
0.3764 over a stable BGE-divisive baseline. A stable balanced leave-one-video-out
method selector further reduces mean $P_k$ to 0.3588 and WindowDiff to 0.3739,
and significantly improves F1@2 over the cross-model method. We position
\lecseg{} as a reproducible lecture-segmentation artifact rather than a
large-scale external best-system claim.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
topic segmentation, lecture video, multimodal fusion, hierarchical
segmentation, local language models, reproducible research.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

The global shift to online education has produced an enormous and rapidly
growing corpus of lecture videos. Platforms such as YouTube and Coursera host
millions of hours of instructional content, yet the majority of videos lack
structured navigation: viewers cannot jump to the specific sub-concept they
need without scrubbing blindly through the recording. This discoverability
problem disproportionately affects self-directed learners and students revisiting
material for examination preparation.

Automatic topic segmentation--the task of partitioning a video into semantically
coherent sections and labelling each with a title--directly addresses this gap.
However, existing systems suffer from three limitations that constrain their
practical deployment. First, most approaches operate on text alone, ignoring
the rich visual and acoustic signals present in lecture recordings
\cite{hearst1997_texttiling,choi2000_c99}. Second, the dominant output format is flat: a single
sequence of non-overlapping chapters, whereas real lectures exhibit a natural
two-level hierarchy (chapters containing subtopics) that mirrors how instructors
plan their content. Third, the few systems that leverage large language models
for chapter generation or titling often depend on very large training corpora or
proprietary components \cite{cao2022_chaptergen,ventura2025_chapterllama},
complicating low-resource reproduction.

This paper presents \lecseg{}, a low-resource lecture-segmentation artifact.
Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Reproducible Low-Resource Benchmark:} \dataset{} contains 30
    public YouTube lectures, 32.52 hours, 419 chapter boundaries, and 904
    reviewed subtopics across five academic domains.
  \item \textbf{Boundary-Selection Analysis:} conservative cross-model
    selection improves $P_k$/WindowDiff over a stable BGE-divisive baseline,
    while oracle and portfolio analyses show that candidate selection remains
    the central bottleneck.
  \item \textbf{Hierarchical Output:} a joint chapter-and-subtopic head that
    enforces the nesting constraint $\mathcal{B}_\text{ch} \subseteq
    \mathcal{B}_\text{sub}$ by construction.
  \item \textbf{Significance and Error Analysis:} paired tests confirm the
    main $P_k$/WindowDiff improvement over the local baseline and expose the
    tradeoff between window consistency and exact boundary hits.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Classical methods.}
TextTiling \cite{hearst1997_texttiling} segments documents by detecting drops in
lexical cohesion across a sliding window; C99 \cite{choi2000_c99} uses a rank matrix
of cosine similarities to find divisive clusters. Both are purely textual
and produce flat boundaries.

\paragraph{Neural text-only methods.}
Neural segmentation methods replace bag-of-words representations with learned
sentence or paragraph representations and train boundary classifiers
\cite{koshorek2018_neural,li2018_segbot}. They outperform classical methods on
several text benchmarks but remain unimodal when applied directly to lecture
transcripts.

\paragraph{Multimodal methods.}
Recent video chaptering work incorporates visual and language cues at much
larger scale \cite{cao2022_chaptergen,yang2023_vidchapters,yu2024_multimodal}.
Earlier lecture-video systems also use slide or visual-change cues as
segmentation priors \cite{tuna2015_classroom,zhang2016_mooc,chand2021_framework},
but those signals are less reliable for chalkboard and whiteboard lectures.

\paragraph{Hierarchical segmentation.}
Prior work on hierarchical topic segmentation is sparse compared with flat
chaptering. Many systems evaluate a single boundary sequence, leaving chapter
and subtopic structure to post-processing. \lecseg{} enforces nesting by
construction and evaluates the resulting hierarchy alongside flat chapter
metrics.

\subsection{Preprocessing}

Audio is transcribed with Whisper large-v3 \cite{radford2022_whisper} (beam size 1,
float16, VAD-filtered). Transcript segments are split into sentences using a
spaCy sentencizer. Visual keyframes are extracted at 0.5\,fps; shot boundaries
are detected using TransNetV2 \cite{soucek2020_transnetv2}; slide text is extracted via
PaddleOCR. Prosodic features (pause duration, mean pitch) are derived from
the Whisper segment timestamps and librosa PYIN.

\subsection{Feature Extraction}

Each sentence is represented by: (1)~a 768-d text embedding from
\texttt{all-mpnet-base-v2} using the Sentence-BERT framework
\cite{reimers2019_sbert}; (2)~a 512-d visual embedding from
CLIP ViT-B/32 \cite{radford2021_clip} averaged over keyframes in the sentence window; and
(3)~a 2-d prosody vector (z-scored). Cosine similarity gap scores are computed
for each modality independently.

\subsection{Reliability-Weighted Fusion (N2)}

For modality $m$ with gap-score vector $\mathbf{s}_m \in \mathbb{R}^{N-1}$,
the normalised Shannon entropy is:
\[
  H_m = -\frac{1}{\log_2 N} \sum_{i} p_i \log_2 p_i, \quad
  p_i = \frac{s_m^{(i)} - \min \mathbf{s}_m + \epsilon}
             {\sum_j (s_m^{(j)} - \min \mathbf{s}_m + \epsilon)}.
\]
The reliability weight is $w_m = \exp(-H_m)$, giving higher weight to
modalities whose scores are concentrated (i.e.\ clearly signal one or
a few strong boundaries). The fused score is
$\mathbf{s}_\text{fused} = \sum_m w_m \mathbf{s}_m \,/\, \sum_m w_m$.

\subsection{Two-Stage Boundary Predictor (N1)}

Stage~1 generates candidate boundaries at all positions where
$\mathbf{s}_\text{fused}$ drops more than $c_1 = 0.5\sigma$ below its mean.
Stage~2 retains only candidates that exceed $c_2 = 1.2\sigma$, or--if the
retained set is empty--falls back to the top-$k$ candidates by score, where
$k$ equals the target segment count.

\subsection{Hierarchical Output (N3)}

Subtopic count is estimated as $n_s = $ (total runtime in sentences) $/\, 4$,
clamped to $[n_c, 4 n_c]$ where $n_c$ is the chapter count. Chapter boundaries
are selected as the $n_c$ highest-scoring positions; subtopic boundaries are
the $n_s$ highest-scoring positions from the full fused-score vector, then
all chapter boundaries are inserted to enforce
$\mathcal{B}_\text{ch} \subseteq \mathcal{B}_\text{sub}$.

\subsection{LLM Refinement and Titling (N4)}

Each predicted boundary is snapped to the nearest sentence within a $\pm 2$
sentence window by prompting Llama-3.1-8B (Q4\_K\_M, Ollama) with the
surrounding transcript context. The same model generates a short noun-phrase
title ($\leq 8$ words) per segment.

\section{The \dataset{} Corpus}

\dataset{} contains 30 YouTube lecture videos totalling 32.52 hours, drawn from
five academic domains: Computer Science (7), Mathematics (4), Physics (7),
Biology (6), and Philosophy (6). Videos were selected to maximise
diversity of presentation style (slides, chalkboard, whiteboard, talking-head).
All 30 videos have native YouTube chapter annotations, used as the chapter-level
ground truth. A subtopic annotation pass was completed by two annotators
following a five-rule protocol (minimum segment 60\,s; 2--5 subtopics per
chapter; short noun-phrase titles; insert boundary when uncertain). The first
videos received dual annotation; inter-annotator agreement (Cohen's
$\kappa$, tolerance $\pm 1$ sentence) is reported in Table~\ref{tab:iaa}.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Setup}

We evaluate all methods on the 30-video \dataset{} chapter benchmark. All
randomness is fixed with seed 42. Metrics: $P_k$ \cite{beeferman1999_pk},
WindowDiff (WD) \cite{pevzner2002_windowdiff}, Boundary
Similarity (BS) \cite{fournier2013_bs}, tolerance-F\textsubscript{1} (tol\,=\,2 sentences),
and a hierarchical WindowDiff (H-WD) that we propose, which applies WD
jointly on chapter and subtopic levels. We report mean with 95\,\%
bootstrap confidence intervals (1{,}000 samples) and pairwise Wilcoxon
signed-rank tests with Holm correction.

\subsection{Baselines}

Classical: \textit{TextTiling} \cite{hearst1997_texttiling}, \textit{C99} \cite{choi2000_c99}.
Neural: \textit{Cosine-drop} (cosine gap + fixed threshold), \textit{KMeans}
(sentence embeddings), and a neural boundary classifier following prior
supervised text segmentation work \cite{koshorek2018_neural}.
All neural baselines use the same MPNet embeddings as \lecseg{} for fairness.

The ablation in Table~\ref{tab:ablation} shows that the project should be
interpreted carefully: some auxiliary cues increase F1 while hurting Pk/WD, and
the best Pk/WD result is a conservative boundary-selection variant. The main
future direction is therefore candidate ranking rather than adding modalities
blindly.

The selector diagnostics add an important limitation. The balanced selector
improves Pk over the BGE-divisive baseline in four of five academic domains,
but Mathematics remains a failure case. A stricter leave-one-domain-out
experiment drops to $P_k=0.4012$ and WD$=0.4103$, so the selector should be
read as a useful lecture-benchmark operating point rather than evidence of
domain-general deployment.

\subsection{Error Analysis}

We identify four dominant failure modes: (1)~\textit{dense vocabulary overlap}
(speaker uses the same technical terms across multiple subtopics, reducing
cosine-drop signal); (2)~\textit{accented or fast speech} (Whisper
segmentation errors propagate to embeddings); (3)~\textit{static visual}
(chalkboard/whiteboard lectures provide no useful CLIP signal); and
(4)~\textit{LLM title drift} (Llama occasionally generates titles that
describe one sentence rather than the whole segment). Domain-level analysis
shows Mathematics and Biology are hardest for the balanced selector, while
Physics and Computer Science are stronger, consistent with the need for
domain-specific boundary ranking rather than a single universal heuristic.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented \lecseg{}, a low-resource, reproducible benchmark and pipeline for
lecture-video topic segmentation. On \dataset{}, conservative cross-model
boundary selection significantly improves $P_k$ and WindowDiff over a stable
BGE-divisive baseline. A leave-one-video-out method selector improves mean
$P_k$ and significantly improves boundary-hit metrics, but its $P_k$ gain over
the cross-model method is not statistically significant and leave-domain-out
evaluation shows weak domain generalization. The main technical lesson is
therefore not that multimodal fusion
solves lecture segmentation outright, but that LECSEG exposes a measurable
candidate-selection bottleneck. Future work should train sequence-aware
boundary selectors on larger chaptering corpora and validate them on shared
lecture benchmarks.
