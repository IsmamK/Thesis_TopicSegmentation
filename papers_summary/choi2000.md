## Problem (2 sentences)

Prior linear text segmentation methods such as TextTiling relied on shallow lexical overlap and were sensitive to noise and domain variation, limiting their accuracy and generalisability. This paper proposes C99, a domain-independent method that replaces direct similarity scoring with a rank-based transformation and divisive clustering, achieving substantially lower error rates.

## Method (5 bullets)

- Represent each sentence as a bag-of-stemmed-words TF vector and compute an N×N pairwise cosine similarity matrix for all sentence pairs in the document.
- Apply a rank transformation: replace each cell value with the fraction of its neighbours (within an 11×11 mask) that have a smaller similarity value, producing a smoothed rank matrix that suppresses local noise.
- Use divisive clustering on the rank matrix to identify rectangular blocks along the diagonal that maximise an internal density criterion; each block corresponds to a candidate segment.
- Select the number of segments automatically by iterating the clustering until a stopping criterion (based on the ratio of within-block to cross-block rank density) is met.
- Evaluate on a synthetic benchmark constructed by randomly concatenating passages from the Brown Corpus into documents with known ground-truth boundaries.

## Limitations (3 bullets, from the paper itself)

- Evaluation relies entirely on a synthetic dataset of randomly concatenated passages, which may not reflect the coherence structure of naturally occurring documents.
- The method assumes a bag-of-words representation and does not model semantic similarity beyond term overlap, so topically related segments that use different vocabulary may not be distinguished.
- The stopping criterion for the number of segments is heuristic; performance degrades when the true number of segments differs substantially from the automatic estimate.

## How it relates to our work (1 paragraph)

C99 introduced the Choi synthetic benchmark that became the de facto standard evaluation dataset for text segmentation through the 2000s, and the algorithm itself (rank-matrix + divisive clustering) remains a strong unsupervised text-only baseline. LECSEG must demonstrate improvement over C99 on real lecture data, where the synthetic-corpus assumptions of C99 do not hold. C99 also highlights the importance of noise reduction before boundary detection — a concern our reliability-weighted modality fusion addresses at the multimodal level.

## Differences from our approach (tied to novelty claims)

- **N1** (hierarchical multimodal): C99 operates on plain text with a single flat boundary output; LECSEG ingests video, audio, and transcript and produces hierarchical segmentations.
- **N2** (reliability-weighted fusion): C99 has no concept of modality reliability; LECSEG learns per-modality gating weights from data.
- **N3** (two-level output): C99 produces one flat segmentation; LECSEG outputs chapter and subtopic levels jointly.
- **N4** (local-LLM refinement): C99 has no refinement or titling step; LECSEG applies a local LLM for post-hoc boundary adjustment and title generation.
- **N5** (LECSEG-30 dataset): C99 was evaluated on synthetic Brown Corpus concatenations; LECSEG-30 uses real lecture recordings with kappa-validated hierarchical labels.
- **N6** (5-metric eval + CIs): C99 reports Pk only, without confidence intervals; LECSEG uses a 5-metric suite with bootstrap CIs and Wilcoxon tests.
- **N7** (reproducibility): C99 code exists but the original evaluation pipeline is not containerised; LECSEG provides a fully reproducible release.
