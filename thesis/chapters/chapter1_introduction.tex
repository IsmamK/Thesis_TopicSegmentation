\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

Online education has quietly shifted how students get to academic content.
Coursera, edX, MIT OpenCourseWare, and YouTube together host millions of
lecture recordings across nearly every academic discipline. Those recordings
tend to be long (anywhere from one to three hours), largely unstructured, and
awkward to navigate without already knowing what you are looking for. Textbooks
have chapters and indexes. Most lecture videos have a progress bar and,
if you are lucky, a handful of rough chapter markers the creator added
before uploading.

Topic segmentation is the problem of automatically dividing a recording into
coherent segments based on shifts in subject matter. In lectures that means
finding the timestamps where one topic ends and the next begins, so a student
can jump straight to the section covering the concept they need to review.
Earlier work in text segmentation~\cite{hearst1994multi,choi2000latent} and
neural sentence embeddings~\cite{reimers2019sbert} gives us the building
blocks, but lectures create problems those methods were not designed for:
domain-specific vocabulary bleeds across topic boundaries, recordings lack
the tidy discourse markers of formal writing, and annotated reference data
is genuinely hard to come by.

\lecseg{} takes on this gap by building a compact, fully reproducible benchmark
for low-resource lecture topic segmentation and then using it to diagnose what
works, what does not, and --- crucially --- why.

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

The growth of online education has run ahead of the tools for navigating it.
Platforms like Coursera, edX, and MIT OpenCourseWare host tens of thousands
of recorded lectures; YouTube hosts many millions more. Most lack any chapter
structure at all, and those that do have chapters are often coarse or
inconsistent. A student who needs the eigenvalue-decomposition derivation
tucked somewhere inside a three-hour linear algebra recording has to watch
from the beginning or guess a timestamp. Neither is a workable study strategy
at any scale.

There are two dimensions to this problem. Practically speaking, recordings
without chapter navigation just get skipped: students give up or look
elsewhere. Pedagogically, navigation is not a cosmetic feature. Being able
to jump to a specific segment, rewatch a hard subsection, and orient yourself
within the argument structure of a lecture directly shapes how efficiently
students use recorded material. Manually authoring chapters for a university's
entire lecture archive is, for most institutions, not something anyone has the
staff hours to do. Automated lecture topic segmentation is the obvious answer.

Real progress has been made in video and text segmentation research, but the
lecture domain has stayed comparatively understudied. Large chaptering
datasets~\cite{yang2023_vidchapters,cao2022_chaptergen} go after general
YouTube content rather than academic lectures. Meeting-segmentation
benchmarks~\cite{janin2003icsi,mccowan2005ami} target conversational settings
that look structurally quite different from academic monologues. Before this
project, there was no compact, reproducible benchmark that combined authentic
lecture recordings with a two-level annotation hierarchy, quantified annotation
reliability, and a full evaluation suite. This thesis builds one and uses it
to understand where current methods break down.

\section{Problem statement}
\label{sec:problem}

\emph{Lecture topic segmentation} means partitioning a video transcript and
its aligned streams into a sequence of contiguous segments that are internally
coherent with respect to some notion of topic. Formally: given $N$ sentences
$\{s_1, \ldots, s_N\}$ with timestamps, predict a set of boundary indices
$\mathcal{B} \subset \{1, \ldots, N-1\}$ such that sentences within a segment
are more topically similar to each other than to sentences in adjacent segments.

The word \emph{topic} is slippery. Semantic-similarity models treat a topic
shift as a distributional change in embedding space. Discourse analysts treat
it as a change in rhetorical function: definition to example, theorem to proof.
Video creators treat it as an editorial call about where to put a navigational
divider. These three notions regularly diverge. An instructor can reset pitch
and introduce a new example (a discourse transition) without starting a new
chapter in any editorial sense, or can open a new chapter without any obvious
lexical break. Because \lecseg{} uses creator-supplied YouTube chapters as
its reference, the benchmark is calibrated to that third, editorial notion.
The implications of that choice are discussed at length in
Sections~\ref{sec:youtube_validity}--\ref{sec:topic_def}.

\emph{Hierarchical} lecture topic segmentation adds a second level:
\emph{chapters} mark major topic shifts while \emph{subtopics} capture finer
transitions within a chapter. The nesting constraint
$\mathcal{B}_{\text{chapter}} \subseteq \mathcal{B}_{\text{subtopic}}$
mirrors the structure instructors typically plan and viewers expect from a
well-chaptered recording.

\section{Positioning}
\label{sec:positioning}

This thesis does not try to compete with large-scale video chaptering systems.
VidChapters-7M~\cite{yang2023_vidchapters},
MiniSeg/YTSEG~\cite{retkowski2024_miniseg}, and
Chapter-Llama~\cite{ventura2025_chapterllama} operate on hundreds of thousands
of videos and are evaluated under metrics that are not comparable to
$P_k$/WindowDiff. They are better systems at scale, and pretending otherwise
would not be honest.

The contribution here is different in kind. \lecseg{} is a 30-video, fully
auditable benchmark designed for diagnostic analysis: every prediction traces
back to its source video, every metric value is reproducible from the same
scripts, and every failure mode is documented. Large-scale benchmarks measure
aggregate performance. Small, controlled ones let you understand \emph{why}
systems behave as they do. The findings in this thesis --- particularly the
oracle-gap diagnosis and the granularity-mismatch explanation --- are precisely
the kind of result that only becomes visible when you can see the data clearly.

\section{Objectives}
\label{sec:rqs}

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1] Which text-embedding and cross-model boundary-scoring strategies
    are most reliable for low-resource lecture segmentation, and at what
    operating points do they outperform classical and neural alternatives?

  \item[RQ2] Does a per-video method selector trained on a small annotated
    fold improve segmentation performance beyond any single fixed strategy,
    and under what conditions does it fail?

  \item[RQ3] Can a two-level hierarchical annotation with enforced nesting
    constraints make lecture segmentation outputs more transparent and easier
    to evaluate?

  \item[RQ4] Which auxiliary signals (including OCR, shot detection, prosody,
    and local LLM refinement) improve or degrade segmentation quality under
    the same evaluation protocol, and what explains their behaviour?

  \item[RQ5] Which performance improvements over the stable text baseline are
    statistically significant under bootstrap confidence intervals and
    Holm-corrected Wilcoxon tests on \dataset{}?
\end{description}

\section{Scope and Challenges}
\label{sec:scope}

We kept the scope deliberately narrow. \dataset{} covers 30 English-language
lecture videos across five academic domains, with Whisper large-v3
transcription as the sole audio-processing step and sentence-transformer
embeddings as the primary feature representation. Everything is evaluated
against creator-supplied YouTube chapter boundaries. Whether those boundaries
are pedagogically optimal is not something we assess. The system is built for
offline batch processing of pre-recorded lectures; live streaming, speaker
diarisation, and non-English content are all out of scope.

Three challenges shaped the design. First, ground-truth scarcity: getting
verified chapter labels at scale means either trusting creator metadata
(which reflects editorial intent, not always pedagogical intent) or doing
manual annotation (which is expensive and slow). We use creator metadata and
document the limitation explicitly. Second, modality mismatch: acoustic and
discourse signals tend to fire at a finer granularity than the editorial
chapter boundaries we are evaluating against, so signals that look useful in
principle can actively hurt your scores. Third, small-sample generalisation:
with only 30 videos, any per-video learning method is sensitive to domain
distribution, and we tried to be honest about what that means for the results.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{\dataset{}}: 30 academic lecture videos across five domains,
    with 419 creator chapter boundaries and 904 human-reviewed subtopic
    annotations, totalling 32.52 hours of transcribed audio. The benchmark
    is the primary artifact of this research: it makes the low-resource lecture
    segmentation problem measurable under a shared, reproducible protocol.
    (Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}, Section~\ref{sec:dataset}.)

  \item \textbf{Candidate-selection bottleneck finding}: An oracle analysis
    shows that near-perfect boundaries already exist in the candidate pool at
    96.8\% recall. This places the dominant source of error in selection and
    ranking, not detection, and relocates the research problem in a way that
    directly shapes what anyone should try next.
    (Chapter~\ref{chap:results}, Sections~\ref{sec:ablations}--\ref{sec:discussion}.)

  \item \textbf{Granularity-mismatch diagnosis}: A systematic ablation shows
    that prosody, discourse markers, BERTopic, and perplexity signals all hurt
    $P_k$/WD because they pick up discourse-level transitions at a finer scale
    than editorial chapter decisions. CLIP is the exception and succeeds for
    the complementary reason. This is the most transferable finding in the
    thesis.
    (Chapter~\ref{chap:results}, Section~\ref{sec:ablations}.)

  \item \textbf{Hierarchical annotation protocol}: A two-level annotation
    workflow with workflow-consistency estimates
    ($\kappa_{\text{chapter}}=0.5351$, $\kappa_{\text{subtopic}}=0.4257$)
    and reproducible tooling. The value is the protocol and the documented
    procedure rather than algorithmic novelty.
    (Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}, Sections~\ref{sec:dataset}--\ref{sec:hierarchy}.)

  \item \textbf{Reproducibility package}: A complete open pipeline covering
    Whisper transcription, spaCy sentence splitting, BGE/E5 embedding,
    multimodal feature extraction (CLIP, PaddleOCR, TransNetV2), seven
    baseline segmenters and four integrated pipeline components,
    $P_k$/WD evaluation with bootstrap statistics, and table-generation
    scripts for all reported results.
    (Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}; Appendix~\ref{app:dataset}.)
\end{enumerate}

The four integrated pipeline components (a two-stage candidate predictor,
entropy-weighted modality fusion, a hierarchical nesting enforcer, and a
local-LLM titling module) are there primarily to enable the diagnostic
analysis rather than for their own algorithmic novelty. Entropy-weighted
fusion is the most distinctive of the four, not having been applied to
lecture segmentation modality weighting before, as far as we can tell; the
other three are engineering adaptations of standard techniques.

This work rests on assumptions that are plausible but not independently
verified: that creator chapters approximate viewer-useful navigation structure;
that Whisper large-v3 transcription quality is adequate for the domains
covered; that BGE/E5 embeddings capture topically relevant distinctions; that
$P_k$/WD correlate with navigation usefulness; and that 30 videos is enough
to surface failure modes representative of the low-resource lecture setting.
We discuss these explicitly in
Sections~\ref{sec:youtube_validity} and~\ref{sec:threats_validity}.
