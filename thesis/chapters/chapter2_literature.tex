\section{Three phases of segmentation research}

Segmentation research has gone through roughly three phases, though the
boundaries are blurry and earlier methods occasionally outperform newer ones
in the right conditions.

The \emph{classical} phase of the 1990s and 2000s was built around a fairly
intuitive idea: topic shifts cause lexical discontinuities, so if the words
used in one passage differ markedly from those in the next, a boundary
is probably sitting between
them~\cite{hearst1994multi,choi2000latent}. That works well enough on
encyclopaedic text but falls apart on academic lectures, where domain
vocabulary (``gradient'', ``eigenvalue'', ``cell membrane'') stays constant
across topic boundaries regardless of what the argument is actually doing.

\emph{Neural} methods, arriving from around 2018, swapped bag-of-words
representations for dense contextual
embeddings~\cite{koshorek2018text,reimers2019sbert}. Working in embedding space
rather than token space makes these methods much more robust to vocabulary
reuse. The practical barrier, needing large pretrained language models, dropped
quickly once open-weight sentence-transformer checkpoints became widely
available, and these methods now set the baseline for any zero-shot
segmentation task.

\emph{Multimodal} extensions pulled in visual, audio, and structural signals
from video~\cite{yu2024_multimodal,cao2022_chaptergen,yang2023_vidchapters}.
Strong results are possible when labelled training data are available. But
these systems have typically been developed on different benchmarks under
different metrics, which makes comparing them difficult. More importantly for
this thesis, the multimodal literature rarely asks whether a given signal
operates at the same discourse granularity as the annotation it is being
evaluated against. That gap, it turns out, explains most of the multimodal
failures in Chapter~\ref{chap:results}.

\lecseg{} draws on all three phases but is not directly comparable to any
single prior system. It occupies a specific niche: small-scale, lecture-specific,
reproducible, evaluated under $P_k$/WindowDiff, and honest about what
did not work.

\subsection{Three competing boundary constructs}
\label{sec:topic_def}

Segmentation research often treats ``topic'' as self-evident. It is not.
Three distinct constructs show up in the literature, and conflating them
causes real methodological problems.

\emph{Discourse transitions} are changes in rhetorical or argumentative
function: definition to example, claim to evidence, theorem to proof. They
are real and detectable (pause duration, pitch contour, and discourse markers
all point to them), but they happen frequently within a single chapter. A
20-minute section on differential equations may contain dozens of such
transitions while remaining, at the editorial level, one chapter.

\emph{Semantic shifts} are points where the distributional similarity between
adjacent sentence windows drops sharply in embedding space. Dense sentence
embeddings capture these well. They correspond roughly to paragraph-to-paragraph
structure, sitting granularity-wise between individual discourse transitions
and full editorial chapter changes.

\emph{Editorial chapter boundaries} are timestamps a creator places to divide
a recording into labelled, audience-facing navigational units. These reflect
deliberate decisions that may or may not coincide with semantic or discourse
shifts. A creator can break early for visual tidiness or late because the
thematic shift only becomes apparent in retrospect.

The distinction matters practically. It predicts which signals will fail on
a benchmark calibrated to editorial chapters, and it explains why most
multimodal signals hurt $P_k$/WD in this thesis. They are accurate detectors
of discourse transitions, not of editorial decisions.
\lecseg{} is calibrated to the third notion.

\section{Classical text-based segmentation}
\label{sec:classical}

\subsection{TextTiling}

TextTiling~\cite{hearst1994multi} finds boundaries at local minima of a
lexical similarity profile computed over sliding windows of $k$ sentences.
The underlying assumption, that topic shifts show up as drops in shared
vocabulary frequency, holds for encyclopaedic text with limited term reuse.
It breaks down on technical lectures. An instructor teaching Fourier analysis
will say ``frequency'', ``signal'', and ``transform'' in every section; the
similarity profile flattens, and the valleys become unreliable guides to
actual topic shifts.

The method is also sensitive to window size $w$ and block size $k$. Our
implementation uses adaptive defaults ($w = \min(w, \max(3, N/10))$,
$k = \min(k, \max(2, n_{\text{blocks}}/3))$) that help on short lecture
segments, but cannot fix the vocabulary-reuse problem.

\subsection{C99}

C99~\cite{choi2000latent} applies a rank transformation to the full
cosine-similarity matrix before using diagonal dominance to locate
boundaries. Ranking rather than using raw similarity values reduces the
distortion from term-frequency outliers, an advantage on lecture transcripts
where a handful of high-frequency technical terms would otherwise dominate
the scores. Like TextTiling it remains a surface-vocabulary method and misses
the semantic distinctions that neural embeddings exploit. We use it as
classical baseline B2.

\subsection{Bayesian topic models}

Eisenstein and Barzilay~\cite{eisenstein2008bayesian} modelled segmentation as
inference in a hierarchical Bayesian topic model, which gives principled
uncertainty estimates over boundaries. The approach is theoretically attractive
but computationally expensive and requires careful tuning; we omit it from
the main comparison but include it in this review for completeness.

\section{Neural sentence-embedding methods}
\label{sec:neural}

\subsection{Cosine-drop segmentation}

Sentence transformers~\cite{reimers2019sbert} opened a practical path past
lexical methods. The simplest approach, computing cosine similarity between
adjacent sentence embeddings and placing boundaries at depth-score valleys, is
surprisingly effective. It operates in semantic rather than lexical space, so
vocabulary reuse that defeats TextTiling and C99 is no longer a problem. And
it needs no labelled training data, which makes it the natural baseline for
any low-resource task.

Its weakness is sensitivity to the choice of embedding model and the threshold
for accepting valleys. Different pretrained checkpoints produce meaningfully
different boundary profiles, and a threshold tuned on one corpus may not
transfer well. In practice this means some per-corpus calibration is needed.
Applied to SBERT and MPNet embeddings, it substantially outperforms the
classical baselines, which is why we use it as baseline B3.

\subsection{Supervised neural segmentation}

Koshorek et al.~\cite{koshorek2018text} trained the first supervised neural
segmenter (TextSeg) using BERT embeddings with a classification head.
Li et al.~\cite{li2018segbot} proposed SegBot with a biLSTM scorer.
Both work well within their training distribution, but that distribution
matters: a model trained on Wikipedia-style text learns the structure of
encyclopaedic writing, not of spoken pedagogical argument. Out of domain,
zero-shot cosine baselines frequently match or beat them. That negative result
motivates the unsupervised approach in \lecseg{}. We use the cosine-depth
variant (BertSeg, B5) as the strongest neural baseline.

\section{Multimodal lecture and meeting segmentation}
\label{sec:multimodal_lit}

\subsection{Meeting segmentation}

Meeting-segmentation methods typically combine lexical features, speaker-turn
evidence, and discourse markers. They are useful reference points for
audio-signal processing, but they target short-form conversational recordings
and were not designed for the structural challenges of uninterrupted academic
monologues lasting an hour or more.

\subsection{Lecture video segmentation}

Supervised multimodal lecture-segmentation systems can achieve competitive
$P_k$/WD results when domain-matched labelled data are
available~\cite{yu2024_multimodal}, which shows that text and visual signal
fusion is worth pursuing. The limitation for this thesis is that such systems
require training data that \lecseg{} simply does not have, and they rarely
discuss whether their auxiliary signals operate at the same discourse
granularity as their evaluation target.

Visual slide-change methods deserve special attention. When a lecture uses
slides, scene changes are themselves editorial decisions at roughly the same
granularity as chapter boundaries. That explains why CLIP embeddings succeed
where acoustic signals fail (Section~\ref{sec:ablations}). For whiteboard
lectures with no visual transitions, purely visual methods collapse, but
within the slide-based subset of \dataset{} they are genuinely useful.

\lecseg{} differs from prior multimodal systems mainly in insisting on
reporting negative results under a shared $P_k$/WD protocol. Most published
systems show only their best configuration; documenting what fails is just as
important and far less common.

\subsection{Low-resource and unsupervised approaches}
\label{sec:low_resource_lit}

Several prior systems target settings closer to \lecseg{}.

Tuna et al.\ (2015) segment classroom videos using visual, audio, and slide
cues without transcribed text. The setting is lecture-specific, but the work
predates dense sentence embeddings and reports domain-specific metrics rather
than $P_k$/WD, so direct comparison is not possible.

Freisinger et al.\ (2023) present multilingual segmentation with unsupervised
rules and optional NeMo LoRA fine-tuning on 34 Portuguese and 96 German
lectures, reporting $F_1 = 67.3$ and $F_1 = 58.9$ respectively. Pause
features help in their setting, but they give no $P_k$/WD figures. That is
consistent with the granularity-mismatch picture: pause features may align
with discourse-level transitions that match their finer annotations, while
misaligning with the coarser creator chapters in \dataset{}.

The AVLectures dataset~\cite{yang2023_vidchapters} covers 2,350+ lectures
across 86 STEM courses and uses a self-supervised alignment method without
chapter labels. It shows that unsupervised audio-visual alignment is tractable
at scale, but it does not report $P_k$/WD and is not directly comparable.

TreeSeg~\cite{gklezakos2024_treeseg} is the closest directly comparable
system. It is fully unsupervised, uses overlapping utterance-block sentence
embeddings with divisive clustering, and reports $P_k$ on TinyRec
($P_k=0.367$, 21 self-recorded lectures), ICSI meetings ($P_k=0.310$),
and AMI meetings ($P_k=0.355$). Its overlapping-block strategy gives richer
contextual evidence per position than single-sentence windows, which likely
helps on TinyRec. The caveat is that TinyRec uses manually verified
annotations rather than creator chapter metadata; whether those two standards
produce comparable $P_k$ values is unknown. \lecseg{} achieves $P_k=0.3588$
with its best selector on a different dataset, so the comparison is indicative
but not head-to-head. Running both systems on a shared benchmark is listed as
future work.

\dataset{} is the only existing resource that simultaneously provides
$P_k$/WD evaluation, a two-level hierarchy with quantified agreement,
bootstrap confidence intervals, paired significance tests, and a full
oracle-gap analysis. That combination is what makes the boundary-selection
bottleneck finding possible to state precisely.

\subsection{Large-scale video chaptering}

Recent chaptering work has moved to industrial scale. Chapter-Gen localises
and titles chapters in thousands of
videos~\cite{cao2022_chaptergen}. VidChapters-7M extends to hundreds of
thousands~\cite{yang2023_vidchapters}.
MiniSeg/YTSEG reports supervised results on 19,299
videos~\cite{retkowski2024_miniseg}. Chapter-Llama shows that long-context LLM
chaptering is competitive at VidChapters
scale~\cite{ventura2025_chapterllama}. These systems are stronger than
\lecseg{} in raw performance and are not the comparison target; their metrics
and training assumptions are incompatible with the \lecseg{} framework.

\section{Hierarchical and discourse-based approaches}
\label{sec:hierarchical_lit}

Hierarchical topic segmentation has been studied under the heading of discourse
parsing~\cite{mann1988rst}. The chapter/subtopic distinction in \lecseg{} is
motivated by that tradition, though we do not attempt full RST parsing. The
nesting constraint
$\mathcal{B}_{\text{chapter}} \subseteq \mathcal{B}_{\text{subtopic}}$
is enforced by construction rather than jointly optimised, a deliberate
simplification justified by the low-resource setting where joint optimisation
would have too few examples to learn from.

\section{Local open language models}
\label{sec:llm_lit}

Open-weight models such as Llama-3.1~\cite{meta2024llama3} make local LLM
inference practical, avoiding the reproducibility and privacy concerns of
closed APIs. Compact open-weight models are competitive on many zero-shot NLP
tasks~\cite{jiang2024mixtral}. We use Llama-3.1-8B (GGUF Q4) for boundary
refinement and segment titling. Whether it actually helps is evaluated in
Chapter~\ref{chap:results}; the short answer is that it does not improve
$P_k$/WD in our experiments.

\section{Four design decisions from the literature}
\label{sec:literature_synthesis}

Each of the four main design decisions in \lecseg{} traces back to a lesson
from prior work.

\textbf{Window-based metrics.} $P_k$ and WindowDiff were developed precisely
because exact-match boundary F1 over-penalises near-miss placements that are
acceptable in practice. For a student navigating a lecture, a boundary two
sentences early is not a failure; the same window of content would scroll past
the boundary under either prediction. Exact-match metrics penalise this case
as harshly as a completely missing boundary, which misrepresents what the
system actually achieves. $P_k$/WD are therefore the primary metrics; F1 and
Boundary Similarity are diagnostic context.

\textbf{Dense embeddings.} Vocabulary reuse rules out lexical methods on
lecture transcripts. An embedding space that separates ``integration as a
geometric concept'' from ``integration as a calculus operation'' can catch the
shift where bag-of-words representations see identical term distributions.

\textbf{Unsupervised methods.} Supervised segmenters trained on Wikipedia or
meeting data do not transfer to academic lectures because the structure of
expository writing is not the structure of pedagogical argument. With 30
annotated videos, training a supervised classifier would be ill-advised;
unsupervised cosine scoring is the only sound low-resource strategy.

\textbf{Granularity calibration.} The most instructive failure mode in the
multimodal literature is signal-granularity mismatch. A prosodic signal that
correctly identifies every rhetorical transition in a lecture will produce
many more boundaries than the creator chose to mark as chapters. This is not
noise; it is a systematic mismatch between what the signal detects and what
the evaluation target measures. Any system targeting creator-chapter
segmentation must either suppress sub-chapter signals or calibrate them to
editorial-level decisions.

These four choices jointly motivate the \lecseg{} design and make the results
interpretable. The contribution is not a new algorithm but a benchmark and
diagnostic framework that makes low-resource failure modes visible and,
crucially, reproducible.
