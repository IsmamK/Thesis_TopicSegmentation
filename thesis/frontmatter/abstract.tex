\section*{Abstract}

Lecture recordings pile up faster than anyone builds tools to navigate them.
Coursera, edX, and YouTube together host millions of academic videos, but a
student hunting for a specific derivation or proof inside a two-hour recording
usually has no option except manual scrubbing. This thesis asks a deliberately
modest question: how far can a small, carefully built benchmark carry us toward
automatic lecture topic segmentation when we have only thirty annotated videos
to work with?

\textbf{\dataset{}} is what came out of that question: 30 publicly available
YouTube lectures from five academic domains (Biology, Computer Science,
Mathematics, Philosophy, Physics), adding up to 32.52 hours of transcribed
audio. We took 419 creator-supplied chapter boundaries as reference labels and
then added 904 human-annotated subtopic labels arranged in a two-level
hierarchy. A second annotator independently worked through all 30 videos,
giving us inter-annotator agreement of $\kappa_{\text{chapter}}=0.5351$
(inflated, since both annotators are reading the same YouTube creator metadata)
and $\kappa_{\text{subtopic}}=0.4257$, which is moderate agreement on a
genuinely subjective task. Throughout the thesis we treat creator chapters as
navigation metadata rather than as authoritative pedagogical ground truth,
which is an important distinction.

The pipeline runs Whisper large-v3 for transcription, spaCy for sentence
segmentation, and BGE or E5 sentence transformers for embedding. We evaluated
eleven method families under $P_k$ and WindowDiff. These are window-based error
rates that tolerate approximate boundary placement rather than demanding exact
sentence matches --- a deliberate choice, since a student navigating a lecture
does not need millisecond precision. Bootstrap confidence intervals and
Holm-corrected Wilcoxon tests back every significance claim.

Two things stood out in the results. The first is about \emph{what actually
limits performance}: an oracle experiment showed that near-perfect boundaries
are already sitting in the candidate pool at 96.8\% recall
($P_k=0.0172$). That means the $\Delta P_k \approx 0.34$ gap to the best
deployable system ($P_k=0.3588$) is, within the framework we evaluated, mainly
a selection and ranking problem, not a detection problem. The second finding is
about \emph{why multimodal signals keep failing}: prosody, discourse markers,
BERTopic, and GPT-2 perplexity all over-segment relative to YouTube chapter
granularity ($P_k = 0.41$--$0.56$). Not because those signals are
uninformative, but because they pick up discourse-level transitions that are
finer-grained than the editorial decisions creators make when writing chapter
titles. CLIP is the exception: slide-scene changes are themselves editorial
artifacts, and CLIP hits $P_k=0.3740$ in text fusion. These two findings pull
future work away from candidate generation and toward candidate selection, and
flag visual slide-change detection as the most promising non-text signal to
pursue next.

\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} topic segmentation, lecture video, sentence
embeddings, hierarchical annotation, multimodal fusion, low-resource benchmark,
reproducible evaluation.
