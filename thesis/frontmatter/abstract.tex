\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Online education produces large volumes of lecture video, but many recordings
remain difficult to navigate because their topic structure is missing,
incomplete, or inconsistent. This thesis introduces \dataset{}, a compact
lecture-video topic segmentation benchmark, and uses it to evaluate text,
visual, acoustic, selector-based, and local-LLM-assisted segmentation signals
under a unified low-resource protocol.

\dataset{} contains 30 public YouTube lectures from Biology, Computer Science,
Mathematics, Philosophy, and Physics, totaling 32.52 hours of video. The corpus
contains 419 creator-provided chapter boundaries and 904 human-reviewed,
LLM-assisted subtopic labels. Inter-annotator agreement on the hierarchical
annotations is moderate at the chapter level ($\kappa=0.5351$) and
fair-to-moderate at the subtopic level ($\kappa=0.4257$), with boundary F1
scores of 1.0000 and 0.7793 respectively. The creator chapters are treated as
reproducible reference boundaries for navigation behavior, not as perfect
pedagogical ground truth.

The evaluation compares classical text segmentation, neural and embedding
baselines, BGE/E5 cross-model boundary scoring, method-selection experiments,
visual CLIP fusion, acoustic pause/pitch features, topic-model and
language-model diagnostics, TreeSeg-style splitting, and local LLM refinement
probes. A stable BGE-divisive baseline obtains $P_k=0.3884$ and WindowDiff
$=0.3956$. The most defensible single global method is conservative
cross-model scoring, which improves to $P_k=0.3713$ and WindowDiff $=0.3764$.
A balanced leave-one-video-out selector reaches the best mean operating point
($P_k=0.3588$, WindowDiff $=0.3739$, F1@2 $=0.0893$), but its $P_k$/WD gains
over the cross-model method are not statistically significant and its
leave-domain-out behavior is weaker. The official claim is therefore a
same-benchmark low-resource improvement and diagnostic analysis, not external
state of the art.

The main empirical finding is a granularity mismatch. Text embeddings and
cross-model scoring are the strongest chapter-level signals; CLIP visual
features can help because slide and scene changes often align with chapter
granularity. In contrast, prosody, BERTopic, GPT-2 perplexity, discourse
markers, and zero-shot LLM segmentation often over-segment because they detect
subtopic or rhetorical changes rather than creator-level chapters. The thesis
therefore contributes a reproducible benchmark, evaluation suite, and
failure-mode analysis that clarifies what lightweight methods can achieve for
lecture-specific segmentation and what must be improved before real deployment.

\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} topic segmentation, lecture video, multimodal
analysis, hierarchical annotation, reproducible research.
